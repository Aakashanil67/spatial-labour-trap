## 1. Verdict in one paragraph

The thesis's literature foundation is substantively sound: every one of the fourteen source documents supports the mechanism the model builds (spatial friction draining a depletable search budget, discouragement as non-absorbing, firms posting vacancies via a profit heuristic, matching efficiency recovered rather than imposed), and none of the empirical findings it leans on turn out to be fabricated or absent. The single biggest problem is not a missing citation but a pattern of **overstatement at the point where evidence is translated into thesis prose**: three of the four calibration moments and two of the three "puzzle" papers (Banerjee & Sequeira; Ebrahim & Pirttila) are characterised more cleanly and more favourably to the thesis's argument than the source text supports, and the single locked calibration moment that anchors the whole spatial story, distance_gradient_slope, is currently sourced to a table (Baez & Kshirsagar 2026, Table 1) that reports population density, not employment, while the paper's actual employment result (Table 5b) is denominated in a travel-time percentile rank the thesis has not yet reconciled with a km-based moment. Layered on top of this are five citation-hygiene errors (wrong venue/year for Shah & Sturzenegger, wrong title for Zenou 2009, wrong page range for von Fintel, "et al." misused for a two-author paper, and the 80%-of-net-income figure attributed to the IMF when the IMF itself attributes it to Shah & Sturzenegger) that are individually minor but collectively read as carelessness under cross-examination. None of this requires abandoning any locked commitment; it requires rewriting roughly a page and a half of the Explainer and fixing the reference list before the moment table is used to calibrate anything.

## 3. What each paper actually says

**Banerjee and Sequeira (2023)**, JDE 164, 103111 (published, open access), reports a randomised transport-subsidy experiment with 1082 Soweto job-seekers (p.2). Subsidised job-seekers travelled roughly 3.5 times more (p.7) and became 9 percentage points (a 77% relative increase) more likely to accept jobs in their own scarce, low-paying township (p.9), yet were not more likely to be employed at endline (Table 6, coefficients 0.006-0.019, none significant, p.10). Method: intention-to-treat OLS on administrative BRT smartcard data plus baseline/12-month endline surveys, three arms (transport-only, general job-search subsidy, control), individual controls and randomisation-date fixed effects. For citation purposes, the paper's own attribution of the null is joint and explicitly unresolved between belief-driven mistargeting and genuine vacancy scarcity (p.9, p.13), not a clean "too few vacancies, and they found out" story.

**Franklin (2018)**, The Economic Journal (accepted-manuscript version reviewed), randomised transport subsidies among 877 unemployed 18-30 year-olds in Addis Ababa across a more job-market-active "board" sample and a poorer, less-educated "city" sample. The board sample gained 7.8 percentage points in permanent work (p.17-18, 40% relative increase over a 19% control mean); the city sample gained overall employment (+8.8pp) and job quality but showed no significant permanent-work effect (p.17). The author attributes this to a dynamic cash-constraint/buffer-stock mechanism, not a static price effect: "cash constraints cause young people to give up looking for good jobs too early" (Abstract, p.1), evidenced by effects growing over time and persisting after subsidy withdrawal (p.3-4).

**Ranchhod and Finn (2016)**, South African Journal of Economics 84(2), 199-216 (published), find the ETI's employment effect on youth in the first two quarters after implementation is statistically indistinguishable from zero: preferred DD point estimate -0.005, 95% CI (-0.017, 0.006) (p.199, p.215), using QLFS repeated cross-sections (667,610 observations, 2011-2014) and a difference-in-differences design. The paper frames this as consistent with a windfall/deadweight story but lists three additional, non-adjudicated candidate explanations (too early, wrong firm targeting, subsidy too small) in its conclusion (p.215).

**Ebrahim and Pirttilä (2024/2025)**, JDE 172, 103394 (published Version of Record, online Oct 2024), use a triple-difference design on PALMS survey and National Treasury tax data (2011-2018) and find a near-zero aggregate employment effect that masks an offsetting gender pattern: employment rose significantly for women (+4.05pp, p.10) and fell for men (-3.39pp, p.10), with the paper attributing this to wage incidence ("earnings increases limit employment gains", p.13) rather than to firms simply banking a windfall.

**Shah and Sturzenegger (2022)**, Harvard CID Working Paper No. 142 (confirmed working paper, not the SAJE published version cited in the thesis reference list), embed South African commuting costs in a DMP-style search model and show empirically, using NIDS wave 2 and NHTS 2020, that total transport cost (direct plus time) averages 57% of net wages for employed workers (17% direct cost alone) and reaches almost 85% of after-tax income for the poorest household-income quintile (working paper p.6). Theoretically, they argue transport costs act as a regressive tax that thins the labour market, and whether this shows up as unemployment (South Africa) or informality (elsewhere) depends on a second, low-informal-productivity parameter riding on top of, not substituting for, the spatial-friction channel (conclusion, working paper p.40).

**Baez and Kshirsagar (2026)**, World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 11285 (explicitly labelled work-in-progress), is a purely descriptive, non-causal study ("Our analysis does not claim causal identification", p.20/PDF p.22) using GHSL population grids, a 2011 census asset index, and OSRM travel-time data across five South African metros. Its headline result is that areas within 5km of a business district have 9,300 fewer residents per km² than the global benchmark (abstract, PDF p.2), and that a 10-percentile increase in travel-time rank to the nearest business district corresponds to a 4.9-percentile drop in employment share in Johannesburg, 3.7 in Cape Town, 6.5 in eThekwini (Table 5b, p.38 printed).

**IMF/Meleshchuk and Schauer (2026)**, Selected Issues Paper SIP/2026/018, use 2024 General Household Survey data, a Theil decomposition, and the companion Manysheva, Mestieri and Schauer (2025) structural spatial general-equilibrium model to show unemployment is over 20 percentage points higher where median commute exceeds one hour versus under 15 minutes (p.6), and that halving transport costs is a slightly more cost-effective inequality-reducing policy than an equivalent housing subsidy in the structural model (p.11-12).

**Von Fintel (2018)**, Stellenbosch Economic Working Paper WP16/2018 (the published SEE 42(2) version was not the file reviewed), uses magisterial-district Labour Force Survey data and geographically weighted regressions to show that the former apartheid homelands' large raw wage disadvantage (108.6%, Table 1, PDF p.13) reverses into a wage premium (-25.6%, i.e. 25.6% higher) once local unemployment and unionisation are controlled for, attributing this to firms never having followed dense homeland populations with new investment (p.4-5, p.20). The paper's entire empirical object is the rural former homelands; "township" never appears in the text.

**Petrongolo and Pissarides (2001)**, JEL 39(2), 390-431 (published survey), establish that a Cobb-Douglas, constant-returns matching function is empirically well supported, with the unemployment elasticity centring in 0.5-0.7 (p.393, p.424) for studies using hires-from-unemployment as the dependent variable (0.3-0.4 for total-hires studies), but stress the function remains theoretically a "black box... driven by its empirical success and lacks microfoundations" (p.425).

**Pissarides (2000)**, *Equilibrium Unemployment Theory*, 2nd ed., MIT Press, is the canonical DMP textbook. Free entry drives the present discounted value of a vacant job to zero (V=0, p.11), yielding the job creation condition p - w = (r+λ)·pc/q(θ) (p.12), which nets out the discount rate r, the separation rate λ, and the vacancy-filling probability q(θ) against the posting cost pc.

**McFadden (1989)**, Econometrica 57(5), 995-1026 (published, poor OCR quality on the copy reviewed), proves the Method of Simulated Moments is consistent and asymptotically normal because simulation noise is controlled by the law of large numbers across N independent micro-observations, not by increasing simulator accuracy per observation (p.995, Theorem 1, p.1013-1015). It separately quantifies simulation-variance loss: one draw per observation gives 50% relative efficiency, nine draws gives 90% (p.1006), a result derived for IID micro-choice observations, not for aggregate whole-economy moments replicated across seeds.

**Kazil, Masad and Crooks (2020)**, SBP-BRiMS 2020 proceedings, LNCS 12268, 308-317 (published), is the correct citation for the Mesa ABM framework's design philosophy (model/analysis/visualisation decomposition, p.309-310) but documents Mesa at version 0.8.6 (p.315), five major versions behind the thesis's Mesa 3.5.1, and cannot be relied on for any Mesa 3.x API detail.

**Wasmer and Zenou (2002)**, Journal of Urban Economics 51(3), 515-541 (published), build a monocentric-city DMP model in which search efficiency declines linearly with distance from the CBD, showing a segregated-city equilibrium roughly doubles unemployment (6.85% to 12.4%, p.531) relative to an integrated one at almost no welfare cost (0.714 vs 0.712, p.532), because the employed save on commuting. Distance is the model's central heterogeneous state variable (p.520); search intensity, not commuting distance, is what the paper holds fixed at baseline.

**Zenou (2009)**, Labour Economics 16, 534-546 (published), extends the same framework to explain black/white spatial mismatch via an exogenous contact-rate difference (Proposition 3, p.542), assuming perfect capital markets and a zero interest rate throughout ("workers are able to smooth their disposable income over time", p.537), which explicitly precludes any budget-depletion channel by construction rather than by omission.

## 5. Calibration inputs found in the literature

**distance_gradient_slope** (employment rate, pp per 10km from CBD). NOT FOUND as specified. No paper in the reviewed set reports employment binned by kilometre-distance. Baez & Kshirsagar's only km-banded table (Table 1) is population density; their employment result (Table 5b) is a continuous coefficient of employment share on a within-city, population-weighted percentile rank of network travel time, city-specific and sometimes polycentric. Two paths: (a) redefine the moment in percentile-of-travel-time-rank terms, documenting the unit change and picking one city or a stated average (Johannesburg 4.9, Cape Town 3.7, eThekwini 6.5 pp per 10 percentile points), or (b) derive an actual km-based gradient from the microdata already on disk (NHTS2020, QLFS, PALMS) and use Baez & Kshirsagar only as directional literature corroboration. This is a locked moment; the mismatch must be resolved, not deferred, before MSM calibration runs.

**discouraged_share** (StatsSA QLFS discouraged work-seeker definition). NOT FOUND in any of the fourteen reviewed papers as a usable external scalar; StatsSA's own QLFS microdata (already in the Data directory) is the correct and only source. Banerjee & Sequeira's 15-month average search duration and StatsSA (2015)-derived 82%-searching-over-a-year figure (p.2, p.4, cited within B&S from StatsSA 2015, not B&S's own primary data) are corroborating context, not a substitute source.

**transport_budget_share** (NHTS-based, definition disputed). Three usable but non-interchangeable external figures exist: Banerjee & Sequeira's 31% of individual income for Soweto job-seekers (p.4); Shah & Sturzenegger's 17% direct / 57% total-with-time for employed commuters nationally, rising to ~85% for the poorest quintile (working paper p.6); Franklin's 12-20%+ of median expenditure for Addis Ababa (not South African, use only as cross-context). None of these is unambiguously "the share paid by the unemployed while searching," which is what the model's parameter c is meant to represent; Shah & Sturzenegger's own paper formally distinguishes τ_w (paid while employed) from τ_u (paid while searching, unemployed) and all its empirical figures are τ_w-type (employed-commuter) figures, not τ_u. The NHTS microdata already on disk is the correct primary source for defining the moment precisely; the literature figures should be used only as plausibility bounds.

**long_term_share** (currently-unemployed with spell ≥12 months, QLFS). Banerjee & Sequeira's 82%-over-a-year / 30%-over-five-years figures (p.2, p.4, sourced from StatsSA 2015 within B&S, not B&S's own data) corroborate but do not replace a QLFS-derived moment; PALMS (already on disk, 1993-2025) is the correct primary source per the Explainer's own table (p.5).

**Other calibration-adjacent inputs available in the literature**: matching elasticity 0.5-0.7 consensus band for the φ=0.6 robustness check (Petrongolo & Pissarides, p.393, p.424); free-entry benchmark specification p - w = (r+λ)pc/q(θ) for disciplining the firm heuristic (Pissarides 2000, p.11-12); Franklin's reservation-wage/impatience shifts after search failure (Table 4, p.7-8) as a qualitative micro-foundation for the discouragement/re-entry rule, though not a fitted parameter. Matching separation rates and formal elasticity ranges specific to South Africa were NOT FOUND in any reviewed paper; these, along with the four locked moments themselves, must come from the microdata.

## 6. Methodological problems, ranked

**1. (Critical, citation-integrity) The distance_gradient_slope moment is sourced to a table that does not exist.** Baez & Kshirsagar's Table 1 (population density by km band) is cited in the Explainer (p.5) as the source for "employment rate by distance band"; the paper's actual employment result is in different units entirely (Table 5b, travel-time percentile rank). Fix: redefine the moment's units explicitly and re-derive it, or source it from the microdata directly, before it enters MSM calibration.

**2. (Critical, mechanism) The "spatial extensions assume uniform commuting distances" claim is backwards and will be caught immediately by an examiner familiar with either paper.** Heterogeneous commuting distance is the central state variable in both Wasmer & Zenou (2002, p.520) and Zenou (2009, p.536); it is what generates each paper's headline result. Fix: replace with the accurate, narrower gap claim, that these papers assume fixed or deterministic search intensity and (in Zenou 2009) perfect capital markets, i.e. no depletable, replenishable household resource stock (p.537) and no discouraged/inactive labour-force state.

**3. (Critical, methodology) SQ1's frictional-minus-frictionless differencing assumes, without demonstrating, that simultaneity bias in the OLS matching-function fit is equal and cancels between the two runs.** Petrongolo & Pissarides's own discussion of this bias (p.420-421) shows its magnitude depends on the underlying hazard rate and the stocks' mean-reversion properties, which will generally differ between a friction-thinned and a near-frictionless economy. Fix: instrument vacancy postings, or stress-test the cancellation assumption directly in the simulation (vary lag/instrument choices and check gap stability) before presenting the differenced 'a' as point-identified.

**4. (Critical, methodology) The MSM loss as specified weights by inverse Monte Carlo (simulation) variance across seeds, not inverse data-sampling variance, even though standard errors are collected for each moment.** McFadden's own decomposition (p.1006) shows total variance is data variance PLUS a separate simulation-variance term; weighting by simulation variance alone means a moment's calibration influence is set by how stable the ABM happens to reproduce it, not by how precisely the real-world target is known. Fix: weight by the inverse of the sum of empirical standard error and simulation variance, or demonstrate simulation variance is negligible at the chosen seed count and weight by inverse data variance alone; state clearly in the thesis which was actually implemented.

**5. (Major, internal inconsistency) The Research Proposal and the Thesis Explainer name two different fourth calibration moments** (wealth Gini in the Proposal p.4, long-term unemployment share in the Explainer p.5). Fix: state explicitly in the methodology chapter that the July 2026 revision replaced the Gini moment with the long-term-share moment, and give the reason.

**6. (Major, methodology) No unconstrained matching-function fit or Wald test of constant returns to scale is specified**, only the CRS-constrained regression (plus the φ=0.6 robustness variant). Petrongolo & Pissarides show reporting the unconstrained fit alongside a CRS test is standard practice (Table 3) and that CRS, while modal, is not universal (US manufacturing sum of elasticities 1.4, p.413). Fix: add the unconstrained fit and Wald test to the matching-function code and use it to justify the CRS-constrained specification as headline.

**7. (Major, methodology) The free-entry test for the firm heuristic ("profit shrinks towards zero as κ rises") does not obviously track Pissarides's actual free-entry condition**, which nets discounted profit against posting cost via r, λ, and q(θ) (p.11-12). A large κ could pass the stated test purely through market congestion. Fix: either incorporate q(θ), r, and λ into the test, or state precisely in the model chapter which asset-value terms the heuristic represents and which it omits.

**8. (Major, methodology) The calibration is exactly identified (four parameters, four moments), leaving no overidentification check.** McFadden's theorem permits this (K=k satisfies the rank condition, p.1010, p.1012) but the paper never discusses the costs of exact identification. Fix: disclose this limitation explicitly and foreground the out-of-calibration validation against Banerjee & Sequeira's null result (prompt 4.4) as the substitute for a formal overidentification test.

**9. (Major, citation) Several public-facing overstatements sit in tension with the thesis's own internal Execution_Plan**, most visibly the "too few vacancies, and they found out" framing of Banerjee & Sequeira (Explainer p.3) versus Execution_Plan Part E item 1's own correction ("do not claim the scarcity reading as fact", line 307). An examiner who reads both documents will notice the disagreement. Fix: bring the Explainer prose into line with the Execution_Plan's own, more careful framing.

**10. (Minor, methodology) The 15-seeds-during-search / 50-seeds-at-optimum choice is not directly licensed by McFadden's 50%/90% efficiency figures**, which are derived for draws-per-IID-micro-observation, not for whole-economy aggregate moments. Fix: justify seed counts with an in-model convergence diagnostic instead, and note separately that common random numbers (fixed seed list reused at every parameter evaluation) is the actually load-bearing methodological strength here, distinct from and stronger than McFadden's argument.

## 7. Threats to the contribution claim

The genuine gap the thesis fills is narrower, and more defensible, than some current phrasing suggests. The claim should not be "existing spatial search models assume fixed search intensity and uniform commuting distances" (section 6, point 2); distance heterogeneity is exactly what Wasmer & Zenou (2002) and Zenou (2009) already model. The claim that survives scrutiny is: these models hold search intensity fixed (or make it a deterministic function of a one-off residential choice) and assume away, or are silent on, any depletable household resource stock that could force temporary exit from search and later permit re-entry; neither has a third, discouraged labour-force state. Zenou (2009, p.537) makes this an explicit assumption ("perfect capital markets with a zero interest rate"), which is stronger evidence for the gap than mere silence, and is worth quoting directly.

Shah and Sturzenegger (2022) are not a genuine rival mechanism to the thesis's spatial-friction story, despite Execution_Plan Part E item 2 initially framing them that way; their own conclusion (working paper p.40) treats low informal-sector productivity as a second-order parameter riding on top of the same transport-cost-thins-the-market mechanism the thesis uses. Part E's own parenthetical instruction to "position the mechanisms as complements" is the correct call and should be followed in the write-up, not just noted as an option.

The beliefs-vs-scarcity decomposition planned for Banerjee & Sequeira (prompt 4.5) is well-posed only if presented as testing which of two readings the source paper itself leaves open (p.9, p.13), not as adjudicating a question B&S has already settled. Overclaiming precision here is the single most likely place a supervisor or examiner who has actually read Banerjee & Sequeira will push back.

On SQ2 and the ETI, the claim that the model "tests the channel at ideal implementation" (Execution_Plan Part E item 3) is a legitimate framing for what the model can show, but it should not be presented as validated against a literature consensus on design-versus-channel failure, because no such consensus exists in either Ranchhod & Finn (2016, p.215, four unadjudicated candidate explanations) or Ebrahim & Pirttilä (2024, p.13, wage-incidence plus skills-mismatch, neither cleanly design nor dead-channel). The thesis should also state plainly, as Part E item 4 already half-does, that a genderless ABM cannot reproduce the specific mechanism Ebrahim & Pirttilä's most rigorous heterogeneity result actually identifies (offsetting male/female employment effects), and that the model's distance and wealth cuts are a different dimension, not a substitute for that finding.
